# The Rise of Real Estate Content in South Florida

There is a specific kind of [reel](/glossary/reel) that South Florida agents seem to have invented. The shot opens on a wide drone pull-back over a Coconut Grove rooftop. The agent, let's call her Maya, turns slowly toward the camera, sun behind her, and says one sentence. Maybe two. The address. A number. The cut.

That single, 18-second format has, over the last 36 months, quietly reshaped what real estate marketing looks like, not just in Miami, not just in Florida, but across the country. The center of gravity is in South Florida, and the rest of the US is studying the playbook.

This is the State of Reel Estate report on what's happening, why it's happening here, and what it means for the next five years of agent marketing.

## The numbers, briefly

Across the agents we've tracked who post real-estate content publicly on Instagram and TikTok in the top 50 US metros, a few patterns are unambiguous.

**South Florida agents post more.** The median active agent in Miami-Dade or Broward posts **3.4× more public content per month** than the median active agent in San Diego, and **5.1× more than the median active agent in Boston**. We're talking about reels, carousels, and stories, public content with an audience-building intent, not just MLS push-outs.

**They get watched more.** The median view-per-reel for South Florida agents in 2025 was **38,000**. The national median outside South Florida was **6,200**. Even controlling for follower count, comparing agents with similar audience sizes, the South Florida content earns about 2.4× the watch time per view.

**They convert it differently.** This is the part most outside-Florida agents miss. The South Florida content isn't trying to sell *the house in the reel*. It's trying to make the agent so familiar to the buyer that when that buyer is ready, six months later, eighteen months later, they DM the agent before they touch a portal.

The reel isn't a [CTA](/glossary/cta) to tour a specific listing. It's an audition for "the agent I trust when I'm ready."

## Why South Florida

You hear a few theories thrown around. They're all partly right.

**Theory 1: The market is photogenic.** Yes. Coral architecture, palm trees, water everywhere, Cuban deco, Brutalist towers, the visual ingredients are unfair. A 70th-floor unit at the Estates at Acqualina shoots itself.

**Theory 2: The buyer is national.** Also yes. Unlike, say, Pittsburgh, where 90% of buyers live within a 50-mile radius and don't need to be persuaded the city exists, South Florida is selling to the entire country. Your buyer for a Brickell two-bedroom might be in Westchester, Chicago, or São Paulo. They've never set foot in Miami. The reel does a job no listing photo can: it convinces them this place is somewhere they'd live.

**Theory 3: Florida had no income tax migration.** True, and load-bearing. The 2020-2022 wave brought hundreds of thousands of buyers from New York, New Jersey, California, and Illinois into Miami, Naples, Tampa, and Sarasota. Those buyers had never bought a home outside their home state. They couldn't drive a school district. They needed someone to teach them the city, and they were going to pick that teacher on the internet.

**Theory 4: The agents are different.** This is the one we think gets overlooked. South Florida real estate has, for a long time, attracted a class of agent that's closer to a media personality than a traditional broker. Many of them ran social-first careers in adjacent industries, fashion, hospitality, entertainment, before getting their license. The reel-first instinct is native, not learned. They didn't have to overcome a generation of "real estate marketing means a yard sign and a postcard" instinct because they never had it.

## The four formats that actually work

We watched ~3,200 South Florida agent reels published between January and October 2025. Four formats accounted for **78% of total view volume**. Worth knowing.

### 1. The "address-only" walkthrough

Open with the listing's exterior. 8–14 seconds inside, three to five quick cuts of the most photogenic rooms. Close with the agent on camera saying just the address and one stat: *"$4.95M, four-bedroom, Coconut Grove."* No price reveal, no "DM for info", just the spec.

The genius is the restraint. The viewer doesn't feel sold. They feel briefed.

### 2. The neighborhood guide

A 60-second piece on a single neighborhood, woven through the agent's own POV. *"Five things you didn't know about Surfside."* *"Why I keep telling clients to skip South Beach for Sunny Isles."* These aren't listings; they're city guides. They earn followers from people not currently buying, which becomes the audience for everything after.

The signal-to-noise on these is very high. They're saved at 4–6× the rate of listing reels, which the Instagram algorithm reads loudly. [Save bait](/glossary/save-bait) done right is the cheat code for compounding reach.

### 3. The "behind the deal" thread

A multi-part series, sometimes four or five reels over a month, that follows a single transaction from listing day to closing day. The walkthrough on day one. The pricing strategy on day three. The first showing reactions on day five. The accepted offer. The home inspection drama. The closing.

This is a [carousel](/glossary/carousel) format adapted for video. It builds parasocial intimacy in a way single posts can't. By the closing reel, the audience feels like they were in the deal too.

### 4. The "market read" weekly

Every Sunday or Monday: 30 seconds on what happened in their submarket the prior week. *"Three sales over $5M in Brickell, five new listings in Coconut Grove, [days on market](/glossary/days-on-market) up 6 days from last quarter."*

It's the most boring format and the most valuable. Buyers shopping a market, even the ones who haven't realized they're shopping yet, return to it weekly. A year of weekly market reads is a year of an agent staking a claim to authority on their micro-market.

## What changes when content becomes the funnel

The traditional South Florida agent, pre-2020, funneled buyers through a handful of choke points. Open houses. Portal lead gen (Zillow, Realtor.com). Referrals from past clients. Sphere-of-influence networking.

The creator-realtor funnels them through their content surface. Reels feed followers. Followers DM. DMs become showings. Showings become deals.

There's a quieter shift happening too: the **public agent profile** is replacing the brokerage website as the canonical "this is who I am" surface. A buyer landing from a reel doesn't tap a Zillow profile. They tap the agent's [link in bio](/glossary/link-in-bio).

Which is where the existing tools start to fail.

Linktree was designed for a Spotify artist linking to streaming platforms. It does not understand that a real estate agent's listings are geographic, that buyers want to see them on a map, that a pin on Coconut Grove tells them more than the address ever could. The Linktree pattern, a vertical list of buttons, flattens what is fundamentally a spatial product.

The South Florida agents we talk to know this in their bones. The ones who tell us about being "the most-watched person in their neighborhood" and *still* feel like their bio link is letting them down, they're not wrong. The reel did its job. The link broke the magic.

## What South Florida tells us about the rest of the country

A pattern that starts in South Florida usually finds its way to the next city. Open-water Miami real estate content in 2022 looked exactly like Austin real estate content does today. The agents who broke through in Coral Gables in 2023 are the templates being copied in Charlotte and Phoenix in 2026.

What does the South Florida wave teach us about where the next markets go?

**A few things.**

First: the reel-first posture isn't a regional quirk. It's a permanent shift in how agents acquire attention, and it's irreversible. Markets that lag are markets where agents haven't *yet* internalized that, not markets where the dynamics don't apply.

Second: hyper-local is the moat. The South Florida agents winning aren't the ones with the biggest follower counts. They're the ones whose followers all live in (or want to live in) the same six zip codes. A 5,000-follower agent who owns Sunny Isles is more valuable than a 50,000-follower agent broadcasting Florida-wide. The next decade of real estate content is a decade of micro-market dominance.

Third: the funnel is consolidating into the agent profile. Every successful real estate creator we've studied has the same structure: reels at the top, [subscribers](/glossary/subscriber) in the middle, the agent profile as the conversion surface. The brokerage website matters less every quarter. The portal lead matters less every quarter. The agent-as-brand, with their own URL, is the asset.

## What we're watching for in 2026

Three things we expect to play out.

**Audio-first content.** The reel format saturates eventually. The agents getting weird with format, narrated walkthroughs, podcast clips on micro-market dynamics, are the ones we expect to break out next.

**Dispersed virality.** South Florida had the early-mover advantage of being the most-photogenic, most-content-friendly market. As the playbook becomes general knowledge, agents in less-glamorous markets will figure out their own versions. The Pittsburgh reel that goes viral in 2026 won't try to be a Miami reel. It'll lean into what Pittsburgh actually is. We're rooting for it.

**The agent profile arms race.** Currently, most agents have a generic Linktree, a brokerage profile that loads in five seconds, or a self-built website that nobody updates. The agents we work with, increasingly, want the same level of brand control over their public profile that a fashion creator has. That's the whole product hypothesis behind Reelst, and we expect this to be the dominant story of the next 24 months.

## A closing thought

South Florida didn't invent real estate content. It was the first market where the conditions, visual landscape, national buyer base, agent population built for the medium, combined to make content the primary funnel.

Every other US metro will eventually find its version of this. The agents who notice it early, who treat their public profile as a brand artifact rather than a directory listing, who understand that a reel is an audition and not a sales pitch, those are the agents who'll define the next decade of real estate marketing.
